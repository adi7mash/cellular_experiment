### 6.6 From pairwise prediction to systems biology

Modeling a biological system, rather than a single target, requires knowing not just whether molecules interact but the nature of each interaction. Existing interaction models return one facet per protein–ligand pair: a binding affinity, or a predicted complex structure. Bonbon instead returns the complete outcome of each interaction — whether the pair binds, how strongly, at which site, through what binding mode, and to what functional effect — a full description of the relationship rather than a single number. At 42,500 pairs per second with cached representations, this complete characterization can be computed for every interaction in a large system, not just a handful of pre-selected pairs.

Section 5.5 provides the first direct evidence that this pairwise completeness composes to cell-level prediction. On the RxRx3 phenomics benchmark, Bonbon's frozen embeddings predict compound-induced cellular morphology changes — a readout that integrates across all molecular interactions in a cell — outperforming Boltz-2 affinity prints that required 100 million co-foldings and additional transcriptomic and PPI signals. The phenomics signature, constructed by the same framework used for mechanism-of-action and orthosteric/allosteric discrimination, identifies which codebook dimensions encode cellular-level information, suggesting that the codebook has organized interaction knowledge along biologically meaningful axes at multiple scales simultaneously.

This result converts the systems biology argument from aspiration to demonstration. Because Bonbon abstracts each interaction into its functional relationship (an agonist that activates a pathway, an inhibitor that blocks an enzyme), these relationships become composable: characterized parts can be assembled into a model of the whole, and complex systems decomposed into the interactions that constitute them. A system's response can then be reasoned about step by step, predicting how introducing a new molecule or protein propagates through the connected interactions — a chain of reasoning that affinity or structure alone cannot support, because they discard the functional nature of each step. The consequence reaches beyond drug discovery: a complete, composable account of molecular interactions is a route to understanding biological systems, and ultimately to reprogramming them, at a scale no single-interaction method can reach.
